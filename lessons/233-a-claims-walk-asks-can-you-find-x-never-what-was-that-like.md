## 233 · A claims walk asks "can you find X" and never "what was that like", so it cannot see the writing

**Enforced by:** CHECKLIST cold-read-both-halves — a cold read is TWO passes by
the same fresh agent, and the unprompted one goes FIRST: use this app as
somebody who was just invited, report what you actually experienced, what you
reread, what you skipped, what you could not tell. Only then hand over the
release claims and walk them. · JUDGEMENT — the second half verifies; only the
first half discovers, and running it second contaminates it with what it was
told to look for.

**Smell:** a cold-read report whose every finding maps to a claim that was
handed over. Also: any review method whose prompt is a checklist, offered as
evidence that a surface is good rather than that named things are present.

**The unlisted app, 2026-09-03.** Fifteen paragraphs of reader-facing copy were
cut from the app's screens in one pass, after the owner read them on a device
and said so. Measured before cutting: 187 paragraphs, 45 over 120 characters,
one at 525 — a form field carrying what a household is, what happens when you
leave one, that changing them is ordinary, and what does not move with you,
above the first control.

Every gate was green. So was the browser walk. So was a device pass.

**And the method that exists for exactly this could not have caught it.** §206
specifies writing each release claim as a place to stand and a thing that must
be visible there, handing the agent only that, and diffing its report against
intent. That is verification against expectations BY CONSTRUCTION. It asks
whether named things can be found. Nobody had ever claimed "that screen opens
with two sentences", so no walk could report that it opened with eight.

§206 half-sees this — it records that a sibling model reads charitably and
shares the author's blind spots, and concludes that its agreement is weak
evidence. The sharper conclusion is about the PROMPT rather than the reader: a
checklist excludes a category of finding before the agent opens the app, and
no amount of reading carefully recovers it.

**Why the unprompted half must go first.** Once an agent has been told what to
look for it cannot un-know it, and its account of "what was that like" is then
a report on the checklist. The order is not a preference; running discovery
second is running it never.

**And it never ran here at all**, which is the finding underneath the finding.
§206 was written from one run on one repo, filed as a CHECKLIST, and a
checklist step that lives in a lesson is a step somebody has to remember. The
method was a year of nothing in every other repo in the family.
