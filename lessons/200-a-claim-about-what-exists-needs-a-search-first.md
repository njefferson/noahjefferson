## 200 · A claim about what exists needs a search before it is said, and the search takes minutes

**Enforced by:** CHECKLIST search-before-you-claim — any sentence asserting that something is rare, novel, underserved, unwritten or unsupported gets a search first, and the sentence then reports what the search found rather than what recall suggested. · JUDGEMENT — recall of a corpus is not a survey of a field, and the gap between them is invisible from the inside. · CHECKLIST both-directions — the same discipline applies to grading a claim DOWN; "the evidence is thin" is a claim about what exists and needs the same search.

**Smell:** "almost nobody", "this is rare", "nobody has written this",
"underserved", "there is no evidence for". Also the encouraging register — these
appear most readily when the sentence is about somebody's work, which is exactly
when they are least checkable and most likely to be received as flattery.

**Three in one session, in both directions.**

**Over-claiming novelty, twice.** A session told an owner that a design method
was something "almost nobody does" — a comparative claim with no denominator,
resting on a sample of *written* text and stated as a fact about practice. Then
it recommended publishing a piece on the grounds that its subject was
underserved. **A four-minute search found the subject written several times
over**, including two syndicated articles and a whole genre around one of its
sections.

**Under-claiming support, once, and this is the direction that feels like
rigour.** The same session graded a widely repeated claim as unsupported and
wrote it into a public document that way — on **one** web query and one metadata
query. Asked whether it actually held, a second search found the claim splits: a
named instance is well quantified (roughly four in five users of one
accessibility feature are not the population it was built for, with measured
reasons), while the general rule has a survivorship problem nobody has
addressed. The published grade had to be corrected.

**Why the second one matters as much as the first.** Grading something down
reads as care. It produces the same defect — a confident statement about the
state of a field, derived from a search too small to support it — and it is
harder to catch, because nobody challenges a session for being too sceptical.

**The rule.** Absence in your own recall is not absence in the world; absence in
one database is not absence in the literature (§15b's logic, applied to indexes
rather than hosts); and the honest form of the sentence is almost always *"I have
not found this"*, which is a claim about the search and is checkable, rather than
*"this does not exist"*, which is a claim about the world and is not.
