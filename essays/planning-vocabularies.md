# Three planning vocabularies, and which one owns what

If you have done both military planning and project management, you have felt
this: two systems describe overlapping things in different words, you know both
sets, and you cannot hold the mapping between them steady in your head. So you
re-derive it every time the subject comes up, and each time it comes out
slightly different.

Nobody holds that mapping. It is not a memory problem. It is a mapping with a
dozen overlapping containers, and the only sensible thing to do with one of
those is write it down once.

This is that, written down once.

## What each one is actually for

**Military planning doctrine** is built for deciding and acting under
uncertainty, by people who will be out of contact at the moment it matters most.
That single assumption shapes everything else in it. Its strength is information
requirements and decision points — knowing what you must be told, and when a
decision can no longer be deferred without losing an option.

**Project management practice** is built for delivering a defined scope with
known resources. Its strength is decomposition and stakeholder mechanics. It is
very good at breaking work down and naming who owns each piece, and it has
comparatively little to say about deciding when you do not yet know.

Neither is a worse system. They were built for different failures.

## Term by term

**The one-sentence statement of what is to be done, and why.** Doctrine calls it
the restated mission — who, what, when, where, why. Project practice calls it the
charter, or the objective. These are close enough to treat as the same thing.

**What other people need in order to act without asking you.** Doctrine calls
this commander's intent: purpose, key tasks, end state, written so a subordinate
can do the right thing when nobody is available to ask. **Project practice has no
real equivalent.** A charter states scope; it does not grant latitude to depart
from the plan when the plan meets reality. This is the single largest gap between
the two, and it is the one that most determines how much comes back to you
unresolved.

**What is known, against what is merely supposed.** Doctrine keeps facts and
assumptions as two separate lists. Project practice logs assumptions, usually in
a combined risks-and-assumptions register, and often does not record facts at
all. **Doctrine owns this outright, and the separation is the entire value.** An
assumption is only legitimate if it is necessary, it is likely, and you have
written down beside it the thing that would turn it into a fact. An assumption
you never revisit stopped being an assumption some time ago and became something
you believe.

**What has to be done.** Doctrine splits this three ways: specified tasks, which
you were told to do; implied tasks, which are necessary but which nobody stated;
and essential tasks, the few without which the whole thing fails. Project
practice has the work breakdown, which is a better tool for decomposition than
anything in doctrine.

**But implied tasks have no equivalent in project practice at all, and they are
where the work hides.** This matters more than it sounds. If you ask each of your
section leads for their part of a plan, you will get specified tasks. You will
not get the work that lives *between* the parts, because it belongs to nobody and
so appears on nobody's list. Doctrine has a named category for it. Project
practice expects the breakdown to be complete, which is exactly the assumption
that fails.

**Who holds what.** Doctrine calls it task organisation; project practice uses a
responsibility assignment — who is accountable, who does the work, who is
consulted and informed. Project practice is better here, and it is worth
borrowing.

**What must reach you immediately.** Doctrine has commander's critical
information requirements: a standing, short list of things that must be reported
to you at once because each one changes a decision you would otherwise make
differently. Project practice has risk triggers, which are much weaker — they
attach to identified risks rather than standing on their own.

The test is the same in both, and it is the part people get wrong: **if being
told would not change what you do, it does not belong on the list.** Putting
merely interesting news on it is how the list stops being read.

**Where a decision has to be made.** Doctrine has decision points, each tied to
the information requirement that triggers it and to the last moment the decision
can be made without foreclosing an option, gathered into a template that lists
them against those triggers. Project practice has stage gates, which are
calendar-driven rather than condition-driven — a real difference, not a
translation.

**Things that must, or must not, be done.** Doctrine separates constraints from
restraints — things required of you and things forbidden to you — and names them
differently on purpose. Project practice folds both into constraints.

**What has been settled.** Both have a decision log. Doctrine also has the order
itself, which is the decision log made binding.

**Where it all stands now.** Doctrine calls it the running estimate; project
practice calls it the status report. Same object.

## What neither of them holds

Both systems assume you will look. The running estimate assumes a battle rhythm;
the status report assumes a reporting cycle. Neither has any mechanism that
guarantees a thing comes back to your attention — they schedule the review and
trust somebody to attend it.

For most of what either was designed for, that is fine, because both assume a
staff. One person running either system alone is holding the return in their own
head, and that is the part that fails first and most quietly. Nothing rings when
an intention simply never resurfaces.

That is a solvable problem, but it is not solved by either vocabulary. It is
solved by a system whose guarantee is the return itself — which is a different
kind of tool from a planning method, and worth keeping separate from both rather
than bolting onto either.

## A working order, for one person with no staff

1. Facts. What is checkable.
2. Assumptions — each written beside the thing that would turn it into a fact.
3. Constraints and restraints.
4. Tasks: specified, then implied, then which few are genuinely essential.
5. Information requirements — what must reach you at once, and from whom.
6. The restated mission, in one sentence.
7. Intent: purpose, key tasks, end state.
8. Decision points, each with its trigger and its last useful moment.

Most of the value is in steps 2 and 4. Almost nobody does step 7, and it is the
one that buys back the most of your own time.
